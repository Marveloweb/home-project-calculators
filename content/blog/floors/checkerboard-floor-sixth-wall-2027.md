For a few years, designers have talked about the ceiling as the "fifth wall." Now the floor is getting the same treatment. On October 25, Homes & Gardens called the floor the **"sixth wall"** and pointed to a new example: the art studio in Kendall Jenner's Beverly Hills home.

The studio, designed by Clements Design, has a floor of **matte tiles in cream, black and red** with hexagonal grout lines, while the rest of the room stays sparse: a white chaise and tan curtains. Grazia picked up the story on October 27, noting that neither Jenner nor Clements Design has said which tile was used.

You don't need a celebrity budget to try a patterned floor. The classic version, a two-color checkerboard, is one of the easiest bold floors to plan and buy for. Here's what's behind the trend, how to lay one out, and how many tiles of each color a kitchen takes.

## Why patterned floors are back

The trend experts quoted in both articles describe a move away from quiet, plain rooms.

- **Pattern over minimalism.** Lizzie Mosley, creative director at Hug Rug, told Homes & Gardens: "Pattern-maxxing has taken over from the minimalism and quiet luxury of recent years."
- **Texture, not just color.** Alex Heslop, a trend expert at Flooring Superstore, said 2027 looks set to take color drenching a step further with "texture drenching," and called the floor a prime place to add dimension.
- **It's practical, too.** Homes & Gardens notes that a busy pattern hides everyday wear and dirt better than a plain floor.

If you rent, the same article suggests a brightly patterned rug for a similar look without touching the floor.

## Straight or diagonal?

A checkerboard can run two ways:

- **Straight:** tiles line up with the walls. It's the easiest to lay and wastes the least tile.
- **Diagonal (on point):** the grid turns 45 degrees, so you see diamonds. Fine Homebuilding notes that a diagonal layout can make a small room look larger, but every tile along a wall needs a cut, so you need more extra tile.

Fine Homebuilding also says a busy floor pattern suits a room with simple walls. If your kitchen already has a patterned backsplash, a strong checkerboard may compete with it.

## How to lay out a checkerboard

A checkerboard shows every crooked line, so layout matters more than on a plain floor. These steps follow Fine Homebuilding's floor layout guide:

1. **Start at the doorway.** The first thing people see is the row at the door, so start with a **full tile** there and push the cut pieces to the far side of the room, where furniture can hide them. For a diagonal layout, start with a **half tile** at the doorway.
2. **Snap your lines.** Snap a chalk line across the doorway, under where the closed door sits, then a second line at a right angle to it, centered in the door opening.
3. **Check for square with 3-4-5.** Measure 3 ft along one line and 4 ft along the other. If the distance between those two marks is exactly 5 ft, the corner is square. Fine Homebuilding says this is more accurate than even a large framing square. Use bigger multiples (6-8-10) on a big room.
4. **Dry-lay before you mix thinset.** Lay a row of loose tiles along each line to see where the cuts land. If you'd end up with a thin sliver at a wall that shows, shift the whole layout a little to one side.
5. **Keep the colors straight.** Stack the two colors in separate piles and check each row before you set it. One tile in the wrong spot breaks the pattern, and it's easy to do once you start cutting.

## How much tile does a checkerboard kitchen need?

Take a **10 × 12 ft kitchen floor: 120 sq ft**. We ran it through the [tile calculator](/tile-calculator/) with these assumptions:

- **12 × 12 in tile** with a **1/8-inch grout joint**
- **3/8-inch tile thickness**
- **Boxes of 10 sq ft** (10 tiles). Box sizes vary, so check yours.
- 10% extra for a straight layout and 15% for diagonal, the calculator's defaults
- A **1/4 × 3/8-inch notched trowel** for the thinset

| | Straight (10% extra) | Diagonal (15% extra) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Area to buy for | 132 sq ft | 138 sq ft |
| Tiles, total | 130 | 136 |
| Tiles of each color | 65 | 68 |
| Boxes of each color | **7** | **7** |
| Thinset (50 lb bags) | 3 | 3 |

A checkerboard is half one color and half the other, so **split the total in two and round each color up to whole boxes**. Here that's 7 boxes of each, or 14 in all, which matches the calculator's total. Seven boxes give you 70 tiles of each color, so you'll have a few spares either way.

On a small floor like this, both layouts land on the same number of boxes because rounding up to full boxes covers the extra. On a bigger room it may not, so run your own numbers.

**Grout:** with those joints, the [grout calculator](/grout-calculator/) puts this floor at about **9.5 lb of sanded grout**, so one 25 lb bag.

> **Fun fact:** Grout lines take up floor space, so you need fewer tiles than you might think. A 12 × 12 tile with a 1/8-inch joint actually covers about **1.02 sq ft**, so a 120 sq ft floor holds about **118 tiles**, not 120. That's before any extra for cuts, which is why the [tile calculator](/tile-calculator/) counts the joint, not just the tile.

## Before you order

1. **Measure each area on its own.** Kitchens with an island, a pantry or an L shape are easier to add up in the [square footage calculator](/square-footage-calculator/). Subtract cabinets and islands if you're tiling around them.
2. **Order both colors at the same time,** from the same tile line if you can, so the two colors match in size and thickness and the corners of the checks line up.
3. **Check the lot numbers.** Make sure every box of each color comes from the same lot.
4. **Keep a spare box of each color.** A cracked tile in a checkerboard has to be replaced with the same color, and that exact shade may not be around in a few years.

Want the look in a room that doesn't need tile? Vinyl and other resilient floor tiles also come in checkerboard colors. The [tile calculator](/tile-calculator/) counts those too: set the joint to zero and ignore the grout and thinset lines.
