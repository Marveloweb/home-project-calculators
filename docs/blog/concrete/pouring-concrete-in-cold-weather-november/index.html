Cold is coming. Can you still pour concrete in November? The 40°F rule, explained

With forecasters watching an early cold turn, here's what concrete groups say about pouring in cold weather, how to protect a new slab, and how much a small slab takes.

By Noah Scott · November 4, 2026 · 6 min read

If you've been putting off that shed pad or sidewalk, the weather news this week is a nudge. Men's Journal reported on October 28 that AccuWeather expects temperatures in the Upper Midwest and Great Lakes "could take a colder turn starting around Thursday, Nov. 5." And on November 1, the same outlet covered forecasts of a record-early disruption of the polar vortex, which the National Weather Service says raises the odds of below-normal temperatures in the eastern U.S., possibly by mid-November.

Nothing is certain. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center still favored above-normal temperatures for most of the country through November 14. But for anyone with a concrete project, the question is the same: is it too cold to pour?

Here's what the concrete industry's own guidance says.

What counts as "cold weather" for concrete

The American Concrete Institute (ACI) defines cold weather as a period when the average daily air temperature is below 40°F for more than 3 days in a row. The National Ready Mixed Concrete Association (NRMCA) adds a second condition: the air doesn't rise above 50°F for more than half of any 24-hour day.

That's not a ban on pouring. It's the point where you need to plan for the cold instead of hoping for the best. ACI notes that the protection you need goes up as the temperature goes down.

Why cold matters

Concrete hardens through a chemical reaction between cement and water, and cold slows it down. Three facts from NRMCA's cold-weather guide explain the risk:

  • Fresh concrete freezes at about 25°F.
  • A 20°F drop in temperature roughly doubles the setting time. A slab that's ready to finish by early afternoon in mild weather can still be soft at dusk in the cold.
  • Concrete that freezes early can lose more than half of its potential strength, and it won't hold up as well over time.

Quikrete's concrete mix data sheet says the same thing in plainer terms: "In near freezing temperatures the hydration process slows considerably."

The 500 psi milestone

The key number is 500 psi. NRMCA says fresh concrete should be protected from freezing until it reaches that strength, which takes about two days after placement. Quikrete's instructions match: protect concrete from freezing during the first 48 hours.

Outdoor slabs face a tougher test. For concrete that will be wet and go through freeze-thaw cycles, NRMCA recommends protection until it reaches at least 3,500 psi, which takes longer.

How to pour in the cold, step by step

If you decide to go ahead, these steps come from NRMCA, Quikrete and Concrete Network:

  1. Never pour on frozen ground, snow or ice. NRMCA says surfaces touching the concrete should be above freezing. Concrete Network warns that a frozen or very cold base "can quickly draw heat from the mix" and suggests warming it with heaters or thermal blankets first.
  2. Order the right mix. For outdoor slabs that will see freeze-thaw cycles, NRMCA recommends air-entrained concrete. Concrete Network explains that the tiny air bubbles give freezing water room to expand, and that preheated water and aggregates give the mix a warmer start. Ask your supplier what they offer.
  3. Don't add extra water. NRMCA advises placing concrete at the lowest practical slump, meaning as stiff as you can work it.
  4. Cover it. Quikrete says to use plastic sheeting and insulation blankets if temperatures are expected to fall below 32°F. NRMCA notes that corners and edges lose heat fastest, so make sure the covers reach past them.
  5. Keep it warm for the first two days. Concrete Network suggests keeping the concrete above freezing, ideally above 40°F, for at least 24 to 48 hours.
  6. Uncover slowly. NRMCA's guide says to avoid rapid temperature changes when protection comes off.
  7. Watch the curing compound. Quikrete says not to apply curing compounds if temperatures below 50°F are expected within 24 hours, and not to use them in late fall on surfaces where deicers will be used.

About accelerators: calcium chloride speeds up setting, and NRMCA says it shouldn't exceed 2% of the cement's weight. But NRMCA is clear that accelerators don't keep concrete from freezing. You still need to cover it.

The first winter: go easy on deicers

A new slab poured this fall will face snow and ice within weeks. NRMCA's guide on surface scaling says to avoid deicing chemicals in the first winter if possible, and to use them in moderate amounts after that. It also warns never to use ammonium sulfate, ammonium nitrate or magnesium-based salts as deicers, because they "destroy concrete surfaces."

How much concrete a small slab takes

Late-fall pours are usually small jobs: a shed pad, a walkway, an AC pad. Here's a 10 × 12 ft shed pad, 4 inches thick, run through our concrete calculator with 10% extra for spillage and uneven ground:

Item Amount
Concrete volume 40 cu ft
With 10% extra 44 cu ft (1.63 cu yd)
80-lb bags (0.60 cu ft each) 74 bags
60-lb bags (0.45 cu ft each) 98 bags
Ready-mix, rounded to the next quarter yard 1.75 cu yd

The calculator suggests ready-mix once a job reaches about a cubic yard, and in the cold that matters even more. Mixing 74 bags by hand takes hours, and the first batches sit in the cold while you mix the rest. A truck delivers it all at once, so you can finish and cover the slab sooner.

For blankets, measure the slab plus some overlap. We assumed 1 foot past each side, so a 10 × 12 slab needs covers for a 12 × 14 ft area, or 168 sq ft. If your slab is L-shaped or has a curve, the square footage calculator can add up the pieces.

Fun fact: A 10 × 12 ft shed pad and a 4 × 30 ft sidewalk look nothing alike, but at 4 inches thick they take exactly the same concrete: 40 cubic feet, or 74 bags of 80-lb mix with 10% extra. That's 5,920 lb of bags. Volume only cares about area times thickness, which is all the concrete calculator needs.

Should you wait until spring?

If the forecast shows nights well below freezing for the two days after your pour and you don't have blankets or a way to keep the base thawed, waiting is the safe call. Concrete that freezes early can lose more than half of its potential strength. If you have a mild stretch, a warm mix and covers ready, a small pour can still go well. Measure now, so you can order the right amount the moment the weather cooperates.
